**Passage One**

When a tech company announces it is relocating its headquarters, the news is typically framed as an economic victory for the destination city and a blow to the one left behind. Tax incentives are credited, job creation numbers are cited, and the familiar script of intercity competition plays out once more. But a growing body of research suggests that this script obscures as much as it reveals: the local economic impact of a headquarters move, whether positive or negative, tends to be smaller and more narrowly distributed than the headlines imply.

Consider the jobs figure. When a large firm moves, the number of positions it brings is often treated as a net gain. In practice, however, a substantial share of those positions are filled by workers who relocate with the firm, not by local hires. A 2023 analysis of five major corporate relocations in the United States found that, on average, fewer than 30 percent of the incoming firm's post-move workforce was recruited from the destination city's labor market. The rest either moved with the company or were hired remotely. This does not mean the move is irrelevant — construction, local services, and the tax base all register effects — but the employment boost for local residents is far more modest than the ribbon-cutting ceremonies would suggest.

A parallel logic applies to the city being vacated. The loss of a flagship employer is rarely the catastrophe it is made out to be — partly because the labor pool remains, and partly because the vacant office space, if prices adjust, tends to attract smaller firms that could not previously afford the location. In fact, several economic geographers have argued that the death of a "company town" can, under the right conditions, seed a more diversified and resilient local economy. The catch, they note, is that this diversification is slow and uneven; workers who lack the means or flexibility to transition between industries may be left worse off even as aggregate indicators improve.

Taken together, these findings do not suggest that headquarters relocation is inconsequential. But they do imply that its significance lies less in the raw headcount of jobs gained or lost than in subtler second-order effects: how the move reshapes the skill composition of the local workforce, whether it drives up housing costs in ways that offset wage gains, and whether the policy concessions used to attract the firm — tax abatements, infrastructure commitments, regulatory fast-tracking — would have generated greater returns if deployed elsewhere.

**Passage Two**

For much of the twentieth century, the dominant model of scientific expertise was straightforward: credentialed specialists working within established institutions produced knowledge, and that knowledge was then disseminated to the public through carefully managed channels. This model, never a perfect description of reality, has been fundamentally disrupted by two forces: the digitization of scientific archives and the rise of networked communities of amateur researchers.

The consequences are most visible in fields where data collection does not require expensive laboratory equipment. In astronomy, for example, amateur observers equipped with relatively modest telescopes have discovered comets, tracked asteroid trajectories, and contributed to the classification of galaxies. In ecology, volunteer networks now supply a significant portion of the observational data used to monitor species distribution and migration patterns. These contributions are not merely supplementary; in some subfields, they have become essential to the research enterprise.

Yet the integration of amateur expertise into professional science has not been frictionless. Established researchers sometimes question the reliability of crowd-sourced data, and professional societies have been slow to develop protocols for crediting non-professional contributions in ways that satisfy both sides. More fundamentally, the boundary between "expert" and "amateur" has become increasingly difficult to define. A self-taught programmer who analyzes genomic data in their spare time may possess more practical expertise in certain computational methods than a tenure-track biologist — a situation that institutional credentialing systems are poorly equipped to handle.

What is emerging, some sociologists of science argue, is not a replacement of professional expertise but a reconfiguration of it: a more distributed, networked form of knowledge production in which the role of the professional scientist shifts from sole producer to curator, verifier, and synthesizer. Whether this reconfiguration ultimately strengthens or weakens the scientific enterprise depends less on the quality of amateur contributions — which, on the whole, have proven remarkably high — than on the willingness of professional institutions to adapt their norms of credit, quality control, and collaboration.
